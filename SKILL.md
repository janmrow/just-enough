# Just Enough

Use this skill as a reasoning lens for reassessing complexity.

The goal is not to make everything simpler. The goal is to find the level of complexity that is justified by the value it creates, the constraints it satisfies, and the risks it meaningfully addresses.

## Core principle

Complexity is not free, but it can be valuable.

Treat complexity as a cost that should earn its place.

Do not ask only whether something can be simplified. Ask whether the value of the complexity is worth its direct, indirect, and opportunity costs.

## Reassess from the goal

Start from the actual outcome being sought, not from an assumption that the current approach should survive.

Existing structure, previous decisions, established habits, and invested effort are not evidence that they are still necessary.

Treat the user's decision to invoke this skill as a request for a simplicity-oriented reassessment, not as proof that simplification is required.

## Examine what the complexity is doing

For the important parts of the current approach, consider:

- what value they create;
- what problem they solve;
- what constraints they satisfy;
- what risks they meaningfully reduce;
- what costs, dependencies, coordination, or cognitive load they introduce;
- what would happen if they were removed, combined, deferred, or replaced with something simpler.

Include opportunity cost when it matters: attention, time, money, or effort spent here cannot be spent elsewhere.

Look for complexity that exists mainly to manage problems created by earlier complexity. When that happens, reconsider the earlier choice instead of automatically improving the machinery around it.

## Compare against a simpler alternative

Form a simpler plausible version that still aims at the real outcome.

Use it as a reference point, not as an automatic answer.

Compare what the added complexity buys against what it costs. Prefer the simpler option when the extra complexity does not create enough additional value.

When alternatives provide similar value, prefer the one that is easier to understand, change, reverse, or remove.

## Use counterfactual checks

When useful, ask:

- If starting today from zero, would this still be the chosen approach?
- What materially breaks if this part disappears?
- Which constraints are real, and which exist only because of earlier choices?
- Is this solving the original need or a problem created by the solution itself?
- Is further improvement necessary, or merely possible?

These are reasoning tools, not a required response format.

## Preserve justified complexity

Do not simplify by ignoring reality.

Keep complexity that is warranted by material value, important constraints, meaningful risk reduction, correctness, safety, security, reliability, legal or regulatory obligations, required quality, or important human needs.

Do not assume away unknown constraints. When simplification depends on uncertain facts or domain requirements, surface the uncertainty instead of guessing.

A complex solution can be the right solution.

Do not optimize for the smallest possible solution. Optimize for sufficient and proportionate complexity.

## Stop at enough

Recognize diminishing returns.

Distinguish between "this could still be improved" and "this needs to be improved."

It is valid to conclude that the current approach is already sufficiently simple, or that only a small part should change.

Stop when the value lost through further simplification would outweigh the cost of the complexity removed.

## Respond naturally

Do not impose a fixed output structure, vocabulary, tone, or level of detail.

Fit the response to the user's task and the host model's normal style.

Be willing to challenge a component, an assumption, or the entire current approach when the reasoning supports it. Be equally willing to keep expensive complexity when it clearly earns its place.
